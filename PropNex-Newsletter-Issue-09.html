The Insider Edge

你有没有发现, 越像广告的内容, 越没人理你?

这不是巧合。人对"看起来在推销的东西", 大脑会自动打折。

但同一个想法, 用另一种方式写, 别人就会读完 — 甚至主动想加入你身边的圈子。这一期分享 3 个写法, 让你的内容不再像"招募", 而是让别家的 negotiator 自己好奇 "我是不是应该也在那边?"

1. 把形容词换成画面

把形容词换成一个具体画面

招募内容最容易露馅的地方, 就是一堆形容词。"收获满满" "能量爆棚" "真的很震撼", 这些字一出现, 读的人会自动略过, 因为广告都长这样。

行销上有个原则叫具体性 (specificity)。人会记住、也会相信的, 永远是能在脑中看见的画面。精确的细节让人觉得你在说真的, 空泛的形容词反而让人怀疑你在夸大。所以方法很简单, 把每一个形容词, 换成一个你真的看到、听到、数得出来的东西。

"今天收获满满" 没有人会记得。换成 "中场休息, 我旁边那位做了十二年的 Leader 跟我说, 他第一年也是一单都没有", 别人会记住, 而且会想多听一点。

2. 句子的主角, 写别人

句子的主角, 写别人不写自己

想知道你的内容像不像推销, 有一个最准的检查法, 看你每一句话的主词。

如果主词一直是 "我" "我们团队" "我们公司", 那就是在夸自己, 读的人会自动打折。这背后是有原因的。研究说服的心理学家 Robert Cialdini, 在他那本被当成经典的《影响力》里提出一个概念叫社会认同, 人判断一件事好不好, 看的是别人怎么做, 不是你自己怎么说, 自己夸自己会直接触发防备。换个方式, 把主词换成一个具体的人, 你写的是别人, 但读的人看到的是你身边围着什么样的人。羡慕是他自己算出来的, 你一个字都不用喊。

与其写 "我们团队平台大、资源多", 不如写 "台上那个人, 三年前还在别家公司, 现在他站在台上, 讲他怎么一步一步做到的"。你完全没提招募, 但看的人心里已经在想, 那我呢。

3. 写门里面的风景

写门里面的风景, 不是门口的合照

得奖照、舞台全景、大合照, 这些站在外面的人本来就看得到, 所以没什么杀伤力, 他们滑过去不会有感觉。

招募行销的研究讲得很白, 超过一半的求职者, 对官方、包装过的内容是带着怀疑在看的, 太精致反而把人推走。真正勾人的是 "原来里面是这样运作的", 那个只有进来才知道的东西。所以发之前先问自己一句, 这个画面, 站在外面的人看得到吗? 看得到的少发, 看不到的, 那才是你的筹码。这也正好是 The Insider Edge 这个栏目的意思, 你给的, 是门里面的视角。

与其再发一张舞台打卡照, 不如写 "我今天才知道, 公司每个月会把 top negotiator 的实战, 整理成一套流程发给我们"。这种话别家公司的人看了会顿一下, 因为那是他们那边没有、也看不到的东西。

当你学会把手上有的、别人没有的东西, 转化成 "门里面的风景", 你的内容就不再需要喊 "快来加入我们"。别家的 negotiator 会自己开始想, 我是不是也该过去那边。

写内容的力量, 不在于你说了什么, 而在于你让别人看到什么。

下一期, 我们继续说, 怎么把公司的背景跟资源, 变成 client 主动找你的理由。

Notice how content that looks like an ad gets ignored the fastest?

It's not a coincidence. People's brains automatically discount anything that reads as a pitch.

But the same idea, written another way, gets read all the way through — and even makes people quietly want in on your circle. This issue: three ways to write that stop sounding like recruiting and start making negotiators at other agencies wonder if they should be over here.

1. Swap Adjectives for Detail

Trade the adjective for something you can see

The fastest way recruitment content blows its cover is adjectives, piles of them. "So much value." "Unreal energy." "Genuinely blown away." The moment those land, people's eyes slide right off, because that's how every ad on the planet sounds.

Marketers call the fix specificity. The thing people actually remember, and actually believe, is whatever they can picture. A precise detail reads as true. A vague adjective reads as a stretch. So here's the move: every time you reach for an adjective, swap it for something you genuinely saw, heard, or could count.

"Huge day today" — gone, forgotten. But "during the break, the guy next to me, twelve years in, told me he went his whole first year without closing a single deal" — that sticks. And people lean in for more.

2. Make the Subject Someone Else

Make the subject someone else, not you

Want to know if your post smells like a sales pitch? Easy test. Check the subject of every sentence.

If it's "I," "my team," "our company" on repeat, you're talking yourself up, and people quietly tune it out. There's a reason for that. Robert Cialdini, the psychologist whose book Influence is basically the bible on persuasion, called it social proof: we decide whether something's worth wanting by watching other people, not by listening to someone brag about themselves. Talk yourself up and the guard goes straight up too. So hand the spotlight to someone else. Write about one specific person. You're describing them, but what readers actually clock is the caliber of people you're standing next to. They run the envy math on their own. You never said a word.

Skip "our team has the bigger platform and better resources." Try "the guy on stage was at another agency three years ago, and now he's up there breaking down exactly how he pulled it off." Not one mention of joining. But the reader's already thinking, so why not me?

3. Show What's Inside the Room

Show what's inside the room, not the photo at the door

Award shots. Wide angles of the stage. The big group photo. Everyone on the outside can already see all of that, so it does nothing. They scroll past and feel nothing.

Recruitment research doesn't sugarcoat this: more than half of jobseekers look at polished, official content and get suspicious. Too shiny, and it actually pushes them away. What pulls them in is "oh, so that's how it actually works inside." The part you only get to see once you're in. So before you post, one quick gut check: can someone standing outside already see this? If yes, post less of it. If no, that's your edge. Which is the whole point of The Insider Edge, you're handing people the view from inside the door.

Instead of yet another stage selfie, try "found out today the company takes what the top negotiators are actually doing and turns it into a monthly playbook for the rest of us." That makes a rival negotiator stop scrolling, because it's something they don't have, and can't see from where they're sitting.

Once you can turn what you have — the stuff other agencies don't — into "the view from inside the room," your content stops needing to shout "come join us." The negotiators at other agencies start asking themselves whether they should be over here.

The power of writing isn't what you say. It's what you let people see.
